Gold Falls Nearly 4% and Moves Away From $5,000: Bond Opportunity Cost Outweighs Central Bank Buying

J5 Trading Strategy Staff · September 30, 2026

Gold prices remain under pressure, and $5,000 is far from being a base-case scenario validated by this week's data. The precious metal is posting losses, and analysts warn that the mechanism driving the price today is not physical demand from central banks, but the opportunity cost against Treasury bonds.

What this week's data shows (September 28–30)

Gold fell nearly 4% on Monday, Sept 29, touching $4,110–$4,112, its lowest level in more than seven weeks. On Tuesday it rebounded just 1.18% to ~$4,184, but the analysts covering the market openly describe it as a simple technical correction, not a trend change.

Peter Grant of Zaner Metals put it bluntly: "Today's move is just a correction of yesterday's losses. I still think there are significant headwinds for gold."

Why it falls even as central banks buy

The apparent contradiction — a falling price while central banks accumulate gold — has a concrete explanation: gold is not being driven by physical demand from central banks right now. It is being crushed by opportunity cost.

Gold is not being driven by physical demand from central banks right now. It is being crushed by opportunity cost.

The 10-year Treasury yield remains above 5%, the highest level since 2007. The dollar is strengthening. And the market is pricing in a ~70% probability of a rate hike in October and ~95% in December.

In that environment, gold — which generates no interest — competes against a risk-free asset paying more than 5%. Institutional investors sell gold to buy bonds. That is what is happening.

The uncomfortable truth about $5,000

$5,000 is not a dead target, but it is not the scenario the market is pricing in today.

Goldman Sachs maintains a $4,900 target for the end of 2026, but in its own September note it warned that if rate-hike expectations intensify, gold could fall to $4,440 — well below its own base-case target. In other words, even the most bullish bank acknowledges that the immediate risk is skewed to the downside.

ING directly cut its forecasts: from $4,850–$5,000 to $4,300–$4,600 for the third and fourth quarters. Deutsche Bank holds on to $4,600, but acknowledges that $5,000 is no longer on the near-term radar.

The structural floor and the marginal price

The structural analysis has a blind spot: central banks buy, but they do not set the marginal price. The marginal price is set by institutional investors who liquidate positions when real yields rise. Central banks set a floor, not a ceiling.

The floor they are setting is somewhere between $4,000 and $4,100. China bought on the dip during the second quarter, and local premiums in China's onshore market spiked, suggesting that the Asian physical buyer is absorbing part of the Western selling pressure.

But that is not the same as saying gold will recover $5,000. It means there is a structural base beneath the market. The short-term direction still depends on the 10-year Treasury and the dollar.

Conclusion

Gold is not in a rally: it is testing a floor while the bond market is winning the game. $5,000 is not a scenario validated by this week's data, and those presenting it as imminent are looking at last year's chart, not the mechanism moving the price today.

Central bank buying establishes a structural base between $4,000 and $4,100, but it does not set the marginal price. That price is determined by institutional investors rotating into bonds with yields above 5%. As long as the 10-year Treasury holds at those levels and the dollar strengthens, the short-term bias will remain bearish.

In short: the floor is below, but the $5,000 ceiling is not on the near-term radar. Gold's direction today depends on the 10-year Treasury and the dollar, not on reserve accumulation by central banks.
